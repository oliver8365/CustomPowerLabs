How to use it

Enter the highest input voltage the regulator will see, the load current, and the ground current from the datasheet at that load. Take the dropout voltage at the same current and at your hottest temperature, since it rises with both. For the thermal resistance, start with the datasheet θJA and then read the section below on why your board may be better or worse. Set the ambient to the air temperature inside the enclosure, not the room.

The equations

A linear regulator drops the difference between input and output across its pass transistor, at the full load current. The ground current, which runs the regulator itself, flows from the input to ground and adds a little more. All of it becomes heat.

PD = (VIN − VOUT) · ILOAD + VIN · IGND TJ = TA + θJA · PD η = VOUT · ILOAD / (VIN · (ILOAD + IGND)) ILOAD(max) = ((TJ(max) − TA) / θJA − VIN · IGND) / (VIN − VOUT) TA(max) = TJ(max) − θJA · PD θJA(max) = (TJ(max) − TA) / PD VIN(min) = VOUT + VDO

Efficiency can never be better than VOUT / VIN. Dropping 12 V to 3.3 V wastes at least 72% of the input power, whatever the regulator.

Worked example. The default values: 5 V to 3.3 V at 250 mA, with 0.5 mA of ground current, in a SOT-23-5 package with θJA = 180 °C/W at 40 °C ambient. PD = 1.7 × 0.25 + 5 × 0.0005 = 0.4275 W. TJ = 40 + 180 × 0.4275 = 117.0 °C, which is 8 °C inside a 125 °C limit. The 85 °C of allowed rise lets the package shed 85 / 180 = 0.472 W, so the most load current it can take is (0.472 − 0.0025) / 1.7 = 276 mA. At 250 mA the ambient must stay below about 48 °C. Efficiency is 65.9%.

Why the datasheet θJA is not your board's

θJA is not a property of the package alone. It is measured with the part mounted on a standard test board, defined in the JEDEC JESD51 series, in still air. JESD51-7 describes a high effective thermal conductivity board with two internal copper planes, and JESD51-3 a low-conductivity single-layer board. Most heat leaves through the pins and exposed pad into the copper, so the board does much of the work.

Your board can be better or worse than the test board:

  • Copper area. A wide copper pour on the pins and pad that carry heat, ideally the ground and output nets, spreads heat out. A small part with only thin traces can be far worse than the datasheet figure.
  • Thermal vias. For exposed-pad packages, an array of vias under the pad into an internal or bottom-side plane is often the biggest single improvement. Follow the land pattern in the datasheet.
  • Neighbours and enclosure. Other hot parts nearby, a sealed box, or a board mounted flat against a surface all raise the effective ambient.

Many datasheets give a curve of θJA against copper area. When the margin is thin, measure: run the board at full load in its enclosure and check the case temperature.

When to use θJC or ΨJT instead

θJC is the resistance from junction to the case surface when nearly all the heat is forced out through that surface, as with a heatsink clamped to a TO-220 tab. Use it to add a heatsink's resistance in series. It does not predict TJ for a part on a board without a heatsink, because in that case most heat does not take that path.

ΨJT is a characterisation parameter, not a thermal resistance. It links the junction to the top of the package under normal mounting, where only a little of the heat leaves through the top. That makes it the right number for checking a real board: measure the top of the package with a thermocouple or thermal camera, then TJ ≈ TTOP + ΨJT · PD. ΨJT is usually small, so the case temperature is close to the junction temperature.

When to switch to a buck converter

An LDO is the right choice when the voltage drop is small, the current is modest, or the rail needs low noise, such as an RF synthesiser, an ADC reference or a sensor supply. Consider a switching regulator when:

  • The dissipation needs a bigger package or more copper than the board can give.
  • The input is more than about twice the output at more than a few hundred milliamps.
  • The product runs from a battery and the wasted power shortens its life.

A common compromise is a buck converter to get close to the output voltage, followed by an LDO with a few hundred millivolts of headroom to clean up the switching ripple. Use our buck converter calculator to size one.

Accuracy and limits

The equations are exact for steady state, but the answer is only as good as θJA, which can be out by a factor of two between a test board and a real one. Ground current is treated as constant. In practice it rises with load, and in some regulators it rises sharply near dropout. Dropout voltage rises with temperature. Short load pulses heat the junction less than this predicts because the package has thermal mass. Thermal shutdown protects most regulators, but it is a fault condition, not an operating point.
