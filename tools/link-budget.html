How the link budget works

A link budget adds up every gain and loss between the transmitter's output and the receiver's input. If the power arriving at the receiver is above its sensitivity, the link closes. The amount left over is the link margin, and it is what protects the link against fading, interference and the losses nobody planned for.

EIRP = PTX − LTX + GTX PRX = EIRP − FSPL − Lother + GRX − LRX Margin = PRX − Sensitivity

Powers are in dBm, gains in dBi and losses in dB, so everything adds and subtracts. Cable and connector losses include anything between the radio and the antenna: matching networks, filters, switches and the feed line itself.

Free-space path loss

Free-space path loss (FSPL) is the spreading loss between two isotropic antennas with nothing in the way. It comes from the Friis transmission equation, and in decibels it is:

FSPL = 20·log10(4π·d·f / c) = 20·log10(d) + 20·log10(f) − 147.55 (d in metres, f in hertz)

Doubling the distance adds 6 dB of loss, and so does doubling the frequency. The frequency term is not the air absorbing more at higher frequencies. It is the receiving antenna: an antenna of the same gain is physically smaller at a higher frequency, so it captures less of the passing wave.

Worked example. A 2.44 GHz link over 200 m: 20·log10(200) = 46.02 dB, 20·log10(2.44 × 109) = 187.75 dB, so FSPL = 46.02 + 187.75 − 147.55 = 86.22 dB. With 10 dBm out, 0.5 dB of feed loss and 2 dBi antennas at both ends, the receiver sees 10 − 0.5 + 2 − 86.22 + 2 − 0.5 = −73.2 dBm. Against a −95 dBm sensitivity that is a 21.8 dB margin. These are the calculator's default values.

How much fade margin is enough

Free space is the best case. Real links lose more to reflections, obstructions and people, and the received signal moves around as things change. A fade margin sets aside headroom for that:

  • Clear line of sight, fixed antennas: around 10 dB is a common starting point.
  • Indoors, or with people and things moving: multipath fading can cause deep nulls, so 15 to 20 dB or more is safer.
  • Wearables and handheld devices: the body can absorb several dB at 2.4 GHz, and more when the antenna is pressed against it. Put that in the other-losses field.

Walls and floors are hard to generalise. Plasterboard costs a few dB, while brick, concrete and foil-backed insulation can each cost well over 10 dB at 2.4 GHz. If the link matters, measure it on site.

Fresnel zone clearance

A radio link needs more than a clear line of sight. The first Fresnel zone is an ellipsoid around the direct path, and obstacles inside it cause extra loss even when the far antenna is visible. A common rule is to keep at least 60% of the first zone clear of the ground, buildings and trees.

r1 = √(λ·d1·d2 / (d1 + d2)) at the midpoint: r1 = ½·√(λ·d)

At 868 MHz over 1 km the zone is 9.3 m in radius at the midpoint, so two antennas at head height across a flat field are already well inside it. That is one reason long-range sub-gigahertz links usually need mast-mounted antennas to get anywhere near their free-space range.

EIRP and UK power limits

Licence-exempt bands limit radiated power, not the radio's output, so antenna gain counts against the limit. In the UK, Ofcom's IR 2030 sets the limits. Two common examples are 25 mW ERP (14 dBm ERP, about 16.15 dBm EIRP) in the 868.0 to 868.6 MHz sub-band, and 100 mW EIRP (20 dBm) for wideband data at 2.4 GHz. ERP is referenced to a dipole, so EIRP = ERP + 2.15 dB. Check the current interface requirement for your exact sub-band, duty cycle and modulation before you fix a design.

What this calculator does not model

  • Ground reflection. Over a flat surface, the reflected wave makes loss rise at 40 dB per decade beyond a breakpoint distance, rather than 20 dB.
  • Diffraction over terrain and buildings, rain fade at microwave frequencies, and atmospheric absorption.
  • Interference and noise rising above the receiver's own noise floor, which reduces its real sensitivity.

For those, use a propagation model suited to the environment, and measure. The presets use typical published figures for common radios and are a starting point only. Check the datasheet for your part, data rate and modulation.
