How to use it

Enter the design frequency, the source impedance and the load impedance as resistance plus reactance. A negative reactance is capacitive, a positive one inductive. Pick a topology. The calculator lists every network of ideal parts that gives a perfect match at that frequency, from the source end to the load end, and draws the one you select.

For each part it gives the exact value and the nearest E24 value. It then rebuilds the network with the E24 values and works out the return loss you would actually get, so you can see whether rounding matters. A low-Q match barely notices. A high-Q one can fall below 10 dB of return loss on E24 rounding alone.

Matched here means the impedance looking into the network from the source is the complex conjugate of the source impedance, Zin = ZS*. That is the condition for maximum power transfer. For a 50 Ω source it simply means Zin = 50 Ω.

How an L-network matches

An L-network is one series part and one shunt part. The shunt part goes across the end with the higher resistance. Across the load, its susceptance B is chosen so that the real part of 1/(YL + jB) equals the source resistance; the series reactance X then cancels the reactance that is left. Pozar gives the closed form for a real source Z0 and a load RL + jXL with RL > Z0:

B = [XL ± √(RL/Z0)·√(RL2 + XL2 − Z0RL)] / (RL2 + XL2) X = 1/B + XLZ0/RL − Z0/(B·RL) Q = √(Rhigh/Rlow − 1) (real terminations)

The ± gives two solutions, usually a low-pass form (series L, shunt C) and a high-pass form (series C, shunt L). With a complex load, both orientations of the L can work, so you may see four. The calculator solves the general case, with complex impedances at both ends, and the Q it reports is the Q of the shunt node including the load's own reactance.

Worked example. Pozar's Example 5.1 matches 200 − j100 Ω to a 100 Ω line at 500 MHz. The two solutions are B = 2.90 mS with X = 122.5 Ω, and B = −6.90 mS with X = −122.5 Ω. As parts, that is a 0.923 pF shunt capacitor with a 39.0 nH series inductor, or a 46.1 nH shunt inductor with a 2.60 pF series capacitor. The textbook prints 38.8 nH and 2.61 pF because it works from the normalised reactance rounded to 1.22. With E24 parts (0.91 pF and 39 nH) the low-pass form still gives 46.1 dB of return loss; the high-pass form with 47 nH and 2.7 pF gives 30.5 dB. Press "Pozar Example 5.1" to load it.

Pi and T networks, and why Q is an input

An L-network has no freedom: the two impedances fix its Q. A Pi or T network adds a third part, which lets you choose the Q. Both are two L-networks back to back through a virtual resistance RV in the middle. For a Pi, RV is lower than both ends; for a T it is higher. Following Bowick, the loaded Q is the Q of the higher-Q section:

Pi: RV = Rhigh / (Q2 + 1) T: RV = Rlow · (Q2 + 1) Qmin = √(Rhigh/Rlow − 1)

Below Qmin, RV would have to sit between the two ends, which neither network can do, so the calculator says so. At exactly Qmin, one part vanishes and the network becomes an L. For complex terminations the Pi uses the parallel-equivalent resistance of each end, 1/Re{1/Z}, and the T uses the series resistance. Each L section has two sign choices, so there are up to four solutions.

Choose a higher Q when you want more filtering of harmonics or a narrower band, and a lower Q when you want bandwidth and less sensitivity to part tolerance. A Pi with shunt capacitors at both ends is the usual output network for a power amplifier, because the capacitors also absorb the transistor's output capacitance and the harmonics see a low impedance to ground.

Bandwidth

Ludwig and Bretchko show that an L-network's loaded Q is about half its nodal Q, so for a high-Q match the 3 dB bandwidth is roughly 2f/Q. The calculator does not rely on that rule. It sweeps the network with ideal parts and finds where the power reaching the load falls to half. To do that it has to guess how the source and load change with frequency, so it treats their reactance at the design frequency as a fixed series inductor or capacitor. A real antenna or transistor will differ, so treat the bandwidth as a guide. If the mismatch without the network costs less than 3 dB, the response may never fall by 3 dB on one side, and the band is shown as open-ended.

Practical design notes

  • Small values are hard to build. Below about 1 nH or 0.5 pF, the pads, vias and tracks are comparable to the part. Lay out the footprints first and include their parasitics, or use a printed stub instead.
  • Check self-resonance. A chip inductor is only an inductor well below its self-resonant frequency, and its Q at the design frequency sets the loss of the match. Look up both in the datasheet at your frequency.
  • E24 is not what you can buy. RF capacitors below 10 pF usually come in finer steps, such as 0.1 pF, while chip inductors follow their own series. Use the E24 return loss as a guide to how sensitive the match is.
  • High-pass forms block DC with their series capacitor, and low-pass forms help suppress harmonics. That often decides between two equally good solutions.
  • Leave room to tune. Fit a pad for a third part (a Pi footprint) even if you plan an L, and finish the match on a network analyser.

Accuracy and limits

The solutions are exact for ideal, lossless parts at one frequency. The tests behind this page check that every one gives ZS* to within one part in a million. Real parts add series resistance, parasitic capacitance and inductance, and PCB pads add more. A match on paper is the starting point, and a measurement finishes it. The return loss uses the power-wave reflection coefficient Γ = (Zin − ZS*)/(Zin + ZS), which reduces to the usual definition for a real source.
