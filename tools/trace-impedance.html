How to use it

Pick the structure, then enter the dimensions from your fabricator's stackup. Microstrip and CPWG traces sit on an outer layer over one reference plane, with air above. Stripline sits between two planes. H1 and H2 can differ, so it also covers offset stripline in an asymmetric stackup. The copper buttons set ½, 1 or 2 oz copper (18, 35 or 70 µm). Switching between mm and mil converts every dimension.

To size a trace, set the target impedance and choose what to adjust. The calculator finds the width, the pair spacing or the CPWG gap that gives the target with everything else held. "Use this width" copies the answer into the form.

How it calculates

All five structures are solved the same way: as a static (quasi-TEM) field problem in the cross-section, using the method of moments. The copper surfaces are cut into short segments, each with an unknown charge, and the charges are found that put every conductor at its own potential. Green's functions for a grounded dielectric slab, and for a dielectric between two planes, include the dielectric and the planes exactly, so only the copper is meshed. One solve with the dielectric and one in air give the capacitances C and Ca per unit length. Then:

Z0 = 1 / (c·√(C·Ca)) εeff = C / Ca tpd = √εeff / c Zdiff = 2·Zodd Zcommon = Zeven / 2

For a pair, the odd mode drives the two traces in antiphase and gives Zodd. The even mode drives them in phase and gives Zeven. Coupling pulls Zodd below the impedance of one trace on its own, so a 100 Ω pair needs narrower traces than two separate 50 Ω lines.

Under the results, the page shows the classic closed-form answer for the same inputs. For microstrip that is Hammerstad and Jensen's model, with their effective permittivity and thickness correction:

Z0 = (η0 / (2π·√εeff))·ln( f(u)/u + √(1 + 4/u2) ), u = W/H f(u) = 6 + (2π − 6)·exp(−(30.666/u)0.7528)

For stripline it is Cohn's exact conformal-mapping result, with Wheeler's equivalent width for the copper thickness:

Z0 = (η0 / (4·√εr))·K(k) / K(k′), k = sech(πW / 2b), k′ = tanh(πW / 2b)

Here b is the plane spacing and K is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, which the page evaluates with the arithmetic-geometric mean. Pairs use Kirschning and Jansen's equations for microstrip and Cohn's for stripline. CPWG uses the conformal-mapping result for conductor-backed coplanar waveguide.

Worked example

The default is a 0.35 mm trace on 0.2 mm of FR-4 with a Dk of 4.3 and 35 µm copper. The field solution gives Z0 = 50.7 Ω and εeff = 3.12. Hammerstad and Jensen agree within 0.05 Ω. The delay is √3.12 × 3.336 ps/mm = 5.89 ps/mm, or 150 ps/inch. Copper thickness matters here: with zero thickness the same trace would be 53.3 Ω. Solving for 50 Ω gives a width of 0.360 mm.

Common targets

  • 50 Ω single-ended: RF lines and most general controlled-impedance nets.
  • 90 Ω differential: USB 2.0, which allows ±15%.
  • 100 Ω differential: Ethernet, LVDS and MIPI D-PHY.
  • 85 Ω differential: common in PCI Express designs.
  • 40 to 50 Ω single-ended: DDR3 and DDR4, depending on the memory controller. Follow the vendor's layout guide.

Practical notes

  • The fabricator's stackup has the final word. Pressed prepreg thickness depends on how much copper it has to fill around, and Dk depends on the resin content. Ask for the fab's stackup before you fix widths. For nets that matter, order controlled impedance: the fab adjusts the widths to hit your target and measures test coupons, usually to ±10% unless you pay for tighter.
  • Dk is not one number. It falls as frequency rises, it differs between cores and prepregs with different resin content, and it varies across the glass weave, since E-glass has a Dk around 6 and epoxy resin nearer 3. Use the value the fab quotes near your frequency. In the worked example, Dk 4.0 gives 52.4 Ω and Dk 4.6 gives 49.3 Ω.
  • Solder mask lowers microstrip impedance. We checked the worked example with a separate finite-difference field solution: 20 µm of mask at Dk 3.5 took 1.4 Ω off the 50 Ω line, and 30 µm at Dk 4.0 took 2.1 Ω. On a 100 Ω pair with 0.15 mm spacing the drop was 6 to 9 Ω, because the mask fills the gap where the odd-mode field is strongest. This calculator models bare copper.
  • Etched traces are trapezoids, narrower on top. With the top 35 µm narrower than the base, the 50 Ω example rose by 0.7 Ω in the same check. A rectangle at the mean width overstates that, because most of the field sits under the trace.
  • Outer layers are plated during through-hole plating, so ½ oz base copper finishes thicker. Ask the fab for the finished thickness.

Accuracy and limits

  • The results are static, the low-frequency limit. They leave out dispersion (the microstrip εeff creeps up with frequency), conductor and dielectric loss, and surface roughness. On thin PCB dielectrics the static value stays close well into the gigahertz range, but check critical lines with a full-wave solver at high frequencies.
  • Against reference results, the field solver matches Cohn's exact stripline values within 0.1%, Hammerstad and Jensen within 0.3% for traces no thicker than they are wide, and an independent finite-difference solution within 0.3% for thick-copper pairs and CPWG.
  • With zero thickness the closed forms agree with the field solver within about 1%, except the CPWG formula once the gap is wider than about H. With PCB copper, the classic thickness corrections for pairs and CPWG can be 3 to 6% out, since they were developed for metal much thinner than the gaps. That is why the page leads with the field solution.
  • Planes are assumed solid and wide. A split or slot in the reference plane changes the impedance completely. CPWG grounds are modelled as wide and tied to the plane below, which needs a row of vias close to the gap.
  • Each dielectric is one uniform Dk. If the core and prepreg around a stripline have different Dk, the result for a weighted average is an approximation.
